Why Your Water Bill Jumped With No Change in Usage

Written by the Delaware 24/7 Plumbing team · Homeowner Guide

A water bill that jumps significantly, with nothing different about how your household actually uses water, is one of the more reliable early warning signs in a home. It's also one of the most commonly ignored, since a bill increase doesn't come with the obvious urgency of a visible leak or a puddle on the floor.

The Test You Can Run Right Now

Find your water meter, usually near the street, in a basement utility area, or in an outdoor meter pit depending on your specific property. Make sure every fixture and appliance that uses water is off, including ice makers, water softener regeneration cycles, and any irrigation system. Watch the small leak-indicator dial, often a red or black triangle, for a full minute or two. If it moves at all with everything off, water is actively escaping somewhere in your system.

This test doesn't tell you where the leak is, only that one exists. That's still valuable information, since it rules out the possibility that your bill increase is from a rate change or a genuine shift in household usage you hadn't noticed.

Where the Water Is Actually Going

A running toilet is the single most common cause of an unexplained bill increase, and it's often silent enough that nobody notices. A worn flapper that doesn't seal completely can waste a genuinely significant amount of water over a full billing cycle without ever making an obvious sound.

An underground leak in the service line between the meter and the house is a less common but more serious possibility, since that water never even reaches a fixture, it just leaks into the ground along the way. A slab leak, similarly, sends water into the ground under your foundation rather than through a faucet, which is exactly why it shows up on the bill before it shows up anywhere visible.

Irrigation systems are a frequent culprit in warmer months specifically, since a cracked underground zone valve or a split line can run for weeks, quietly soaking a section of yard, before anyone notices the grass looking unusually green in one spot.

Why Del-Co and City of Delaware Bills Make This Easier to Catch

Both Del-Co Water Company and the City of Delaware Water Division provide detailed usage history on a typical bill, which makes comparing month to month straightforward. A homeowner who checks that history regularly catches a leak-driven increase faster than one who only glances at the total amount due each month.

What to Do Once You've Confirmed a Leak Exists

Check the easy, visible possibilities first. Lift the toilet tank lid and listen for water running when nothing has been flushed, or add a few drops of food coloring to the tank and check the bowl a few minutes later without flushing. Colored water in the bowl means the flapper is leaking.

If the toilets check out and the meter is still moving, the leak is more likely to be a hidden supply line, a slab leak, or an irrigation line, all of which need professional leak detection equipment to locate precisely. Acoustic and thermal equipment finds the specific location without guessing where to start cutting or digging.

How Professional Leak Detection Actually Narrows It Down

Pressure isolation testing is often the first real step, since it lets a technician shut off sections of your home's plumbing one at a time and watch how the system pressure responds. That narrows a whole-house mystery down to a specific zone before any acoustic or thermal equipment even comes out.

Once the zone is narrowed, acoustic listening equipment picks up the distinct sound of water escaping a pressurized line, and thermal imaging reveals temperature differences where water is saturating a wall or slab. Combined, these tools usually confirm a leak's location within a small, specific area, which is what makes a targeted repair possible instead of an expensive guess.

Preventing the Next Unexplained Spike

Reading your meter on the same day each month and comparing it against your actual bill catches a developing problem faster than waiting for the bill itself to reflect a full cycle of waste. Toilets are worth a quick check every few months regardless, given how easily a flapper leak goes unnoticed, and irrigation systems are worth a visual inspection each spring before the watering season really ramps up.

What Documenting a Confirmed Leak Does for You

Once a leak is confirmed and repaired, some water providers, including in parts of Delaware County, will adjust a billing cycle that showed unusually high usage tied to a documented leak. This especially applies for a hidden leak like a slab or underground line that wasn't visible or preventable. Keeping the repair invoice and any leak detection report on file gives you the documentation needed to request that adjustment rather than simply absorbing the higher bill.

Rural Properties and Longer Service Runs

A rural Delaware County property with a longer service line run from the road to the house has more buried pipe that could potentially develop a leak compared to a home closer to the street. That longer run isn't inherently more failure-prone, but it does mean a leak detection technician has more ground to cover when narrowing down a location, which is part of why pressure isolation testing matters even more on these properties before bringing out acoustic equipment.

One More Thing Worth Checking: Your Water Softener

A water softener stuck in a regeneration loop, or one with a malfunctioning control valve, can waste a real amount of water without anyone noticing, since the system runs its cycle automatically in the background. If your meter test shows movement and your toilets check out, a softener malfunction is worth ruling out before assuming the leak is somewhere more serious like a slab or underground line. Any resulting repair follows the same registration standard set by Ohio House Bill 487.

Common Questions About Water Bill Spikes

How much of a bill increase is actually significant? A jump of 20 percent or more with no explained change in usage is worth investigating. Smaller month-to-month variation is normal and often tied to seasonal factors like more laundry or additional houseguests.

Can a water softener cause a higher bill? Yes, but predictably. A softener regenerates on a schedule and uses a modest, consistent amount of water each cycle. A sudden, unexplained jump beyond that normal pattern points to something else, not the softener itself.

Will the water company tell me if there's a leak? Some providers flag unusually high consumption and may reach out, but this isn't guaranteed or immediate. Checking your own meter and bill history regularly catches a leak faster than waiting for the utility to notice.
