Hard Water in Delaware, Ohio: What It's Actually Doing to Your Plumbing

Written by the Delaware 24/7 Plumbing team · Water Quality Guide

If you've noticed spots on your dishes that won't rinse away, soap that won't lather easily, or a water heater that seems to be failing earlier than it should, hard water is very likely part of the story. Del-Co Water Company and the City of Delaware Water Division both supply water with genuine, measurable mineral hardness, and it's not a minor cosmetic issue.

What Hardness Actually Means

Hard water contains dissolved calcium and magnesium, which is common throughout central Ohio's groundwater. It isn't a health concern, but it changes how water behaves. Soap and shampoo don't lather as easily, since the minerals interfere with the chemistry that creates lather. Dishes and glassware come out of the dishwasher with visible spotting, since mineral deposits are left behind as water evaporates.

The Real Cost Inside Your Plumbing

Inside a water heater, hardness minerals settle at the bottom of the tank over years and insulate the heating element from the water above it. That forces the unit to run longer to reach the same temperature, and it's a real reason a tank rated for 10 to 12 years often shows meaningful trouble by year 8 or 9 in this area specifically.

Faucets, showerheads, and fixture aerators accumulate the same mineral buildup over time, gradually restricting water flow even when nothing has technically failed. A showerhead that's lost real pressure over a year or two is frequently a hardness problem rather than a plumbing problem elsewhere in the house.

What a Softener Actually Fixes

A water softener exchanges hardness minerals for sodium or potassium through a resin bed, which delivers water that lathers normally and stops leaving mineral residue on fixtures and dishes. Sizing matters here. A softener too small for your household regenerates constantly and wears out faster, while one too large wastes salt and water on cycles it doesn't need.

If you're not sure whether your specific home would benefit, a real water hardness test during a service visit gives you an actual number rather than a guess, and that number is what determines whether a softener makes sense and what size fits your household.

Signs Your Current Softener Isn't Keeping Up

A softener that's stopped regenerating on its normal schedule, or a household that suddenly notices water feeling hard again after years of it feeling fine, usually points to a specific, fixable issue. A depleted or bridged salt supply in the brine tank is the most common cause, followed by a control valve that's stopped cycling correctly. Both are typically straightforward repairs on a system that's otherwise in reasonable shape.

A system well past 10 to 15 years old, especially one that's needed more than one repair in the past year or two, is a more realistic candidate for replacement than continued piecemeal fixes. The resin bed itself does eventually wear out and lose its exchange capacity permanently.

A Note on Sodium Content

A salt-based softener adds a small, measurable amount of sodium to treated water through the ion exchange process. For most households this is a non-issue, but if anyone in the home is on a sodium-restricted diet, it's worth a real conversation before installation. A potassium chloride alternative, or a separate unsoftened tap kept for drinking and cooking water, are both legitimate options worth discussing rather than assuming softened water is universally fine for every household.

What About Well Water on the County's Edges

Homes on private wells in Delaware County's more rural townships face a related but genuinely different water quality picture than homes on Del-Co or city water. Well water can carry iron, sulfur, or a different hardness level entirely depending on the specific aquifer it draws from, and treating well water the same as municipal water without testing it first is a real mistake. A well water test identifies exactly what's present, since a system designed for municipal hardness alone won't address iron staining or a sulfur odor if those are also part of the picture.

What to Check Before You Call

Before scheduling a professional test, a few household observations give you a rough sense of whether hardness is genuinely significant in your home. Soap that won't work up a real lather even with plenty of product, a bathtub ring that forms quickly after cleaning, and white or chalky buildup around faucet aerators and showerheads are all consistent with meaningfully hard water. None of these replace an actual hardness test, but they're a reasonable first signal that it's worth scheduling one.

Softening vs. Filtering: Not the Same Fix

A common point of confusion is treating a softener as a cure for every water quality complaint. A softener addresses hardness specifically. It does nothing for chlorine taste, sediment, or odor, which are separate issues needing a filtration system instead. Some households genuinely benefit from both installed together, but installing a softener to fix a chlorine smell leaves the actual complaint unresolved, since it was never designed to address that particular problem.

Common Questions About Hard Water in Delaware

Is Delaware's water safe to drink even though it's hard? Yes. Hardness is a mineral content issue, not a safety issue, and hard water is perfectly safe to drink. The concern with hardness is entirely about its effect on fixtures, appliances, and how soap and detergent perform, not health.

How do I know my exact water hardness number? A real test during a service visit measures hardness in grains per gallon, which is the standard unit used to size a softener correctly and accurately. Without that specific number, sizing a system is a guess rather than a real calculation.

Does boiling water remove hardness? No, boiling does not meaningfully remove calcium and magnesium from water. It can cause some scale to precipitate out in a kettle over time, which is actually a visible example of the same mineral buildup happening throughout your plumbing, just concentrated in one visible spot.

Will a softener fix low water pressure caused by mineral buildup? A new softener stops further buildup from accumulating, but it won't reverse scale that's already narrowed a pipe or clogged a fixture. Existing buildup in fixtures usually needs cleaning or replacement separately, alongside the softener addressing the ongoing cause.

Do renters in Delaware deal with hard water the same as homeowners? Yes, the water supply is the same regardless of ownership. A renter noticing hardness symptoms can reasonably raise it with a landlord, though installing a whole-house softener is typically a landlord's decision given it's a permanent plumbing addition to the property.
