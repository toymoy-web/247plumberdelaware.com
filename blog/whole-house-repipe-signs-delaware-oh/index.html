Signs Your Delaware Home May Need a Whole-House Repipe

Written by the Delaware 24/7 Plumbing team · Homeowner Guide

A whole-house repipe is a significant decision, and most homeowners understandably want to avoid it until it's genuinely necessary. The challenge is recognizing the difference between a single, isolated pipe issue and a real, system-wide problem before it becomes an emergency. Here are the actual signs worth taking seriously.

Sign One: Recurring Leaks in Different Locations

A single pinhole leak, fixed once, that never recurs is a normal, isolated event. A second leak in a completely different location within a year or two of the first is a meaningfully different signal. It suggests the underlying pipe material throughout the house is reaching the same age-related failure point, not that you've had unusually bad luck twice.

Sign Two: Discolored Water, Especially After Sitting

Rust-colored or brownish water, particularly noticeable first thing in the morning or after the house has been unused for a day, points toward internal corrosion inside galvanized pipe. This happens as the zinc coating that originally protected the steel pipe wears away over decades, exposing the steel underneath to direct corrosion from the water itself.

Sign Three: Noticeably Low Water Pressure Throughout the House

Low pressure at one specific fixture usually points to a local issue, like a clogged aerator or a partially closed shutoff valve. Low pressure at every fixture throughout the entire house, especially if it's developed gradually over years rather than appearing suddenly, points toward the supply pipe's interior diameter narrowing from accumulated corrosion and mineral buildup throughout the system.

Sign Four: Visible Pipe Material and Age

If you can see exposed pipe in a basement or utility area, dull gray galvanized steel with visible threading at joints is the material most associated with this whole conversation. Homes built in Delaware before the 1960s, particularly near downtown and around Ohio Wesleyan University, are the most likely candidates for still having original galvanized supply lines rather than a prior updated system.

What a Repipe Actually Involves

A whole-home repipe replaces the home's supply lines throughout with modern copper or PEX, done in stages so most of the house retains some water access during the multi-day project rather than everyone going without water entirely. Most residential repipes take 2 to 5 days depending on home size and how accessible the existing pipe runs are.

Copper offers a long track record and handles heat well, though it costs more and takes longer to install. PEX is flexible, faster to install with fewer required joints, and typically costs less, though some homeowners prefer copper's familiarity and resale-value reputation. Either material is a reasonable, durable choice depending on your home and budget.

When It's Actually a Targeted Repair Instead

A single, confirmed leak in an otherwise sound system, with no other symptoms like widespread discoloration or pressure loss, is genuinely still a candidate for a targeted repair rather than a full repipe. Not every older home with one pipe issue needs the full project, and an honest evaluation should distinguish between the two rather than defaulting to whichever is more expensive.

Getting an Honest Second Opinion

A repipe is a significant enough investment that a second opinion is reasonable before committing, especially if the first assessment recommends the full project based on a single symptom rather than the combination of signs described here. A plumber who walks through the specific evidence, not just a general recommendation, gives you what you need to feel confident in the decision either way.

What Happens to Fixtures During the Project

Most repipes connect into your existing fixtures rather than requiring their replacement, so faucets, toilets, and shower valves generally stay as they are unless you're planning to update them separately. Older fixtures with worn shutoff valves are a reasonable candidate to replace at the same time, since the access is already open and doing it later as a separate project means paying for that access twice.

How a Repipe Affects Homeowners Insurance

A documented whole-home repipe, replacing aging galvanized pipe with modern copper or PEX, is a genuine capital improvement that some insurers will factor into your policy, occasionally resulting in a modest premium reduction since the risk of a future pipe-related claim drops meaningfully. Checking with your specific insurer after a repipe is completed is worth the phone call, since the improvement is real and documentable, and it's performed under the same state registration standard set by Ohio House Bill 487 as any other plumbing work.

Timing a Repipe Around Other Home Projects

If you're already planning a kitchen or bathroom renovation that involves opening walls, coordinating a repipe alongside that project meaningfully reduces total disruption and cost compared to doing each as a separate, later project. Discussing your broader renovation timeline with us before either project starts lets us plan around what's already going to be open rather than creating new access points unnecessarily.

Living Through a Repipe Without Major Disruption

Most families continue living in the home throughout a repipe, since water is restored to most fixtures each evening even during a multi-day project staged in sections. Planning around the specific rooms losing water access on a given day, rather than assuming the whole house goes without water for the entire project, makes the actual experience considerably more manageable than most homeowners initially expect going into it.

Planning Ahead Rather Than Waiting for Failure

For a home showing early signs like slightly discolored water or gradually declining pressure, but nothing severe yet, planning a repipe proactively on your own timeline is almost always less disruptive and often less expensive than waiting for a genuine emergency to force the decision. Scheduling it during a renovation or a period when you're already prepared for some household disruption minimizes the real inconvenience of the project.

Common Questions About Repiping

How much does a whole-house repipe typically cost? It varies significantly with home size, number of fixtures, and how accessible the existing pipe runs are, generally ranging from several thousand dollars for a smaller home to considerably more for a larger property with difficult access.

Will my water pressure improve after a repipe? Often significantly, yes, since new pipe with a full, clean interior diameter restores pressure closer to what your fixtures were originally designed to deliver, especially if the old pipe had narrowed substantially from corrosion.

Can I repipe just part of my home instead of everything? Sometimes, if problems are genuinely concentrated in one area rather than spread throughout the property. A real assessment of where issues have actually occurred determines whether a partial or full repipe makes more sense for your specific situation.

Does a repipe require a permit and inspection? Yes, most jurisdictions require a permit for this scope of plumbing work, and we handle that process as part of the project rather than leaving it for you to navigate separately.
