Sump Pump Failure During a Storm in Delaware County

Written by the Delaware 24/7 Plumbing team · Seasonal Guide

A sump pump exists for exactly one moment: the storm that raises groundwater faster than it can naturally drain away. When that moment arrives and the pump fails, the result is often the most expensive basement damage a Delaware County homeowner will ever deal with, precisely because it happens at the worst possible time.

Why Failure Happens During the Storm, Not Before

A sump pump can sit unused for weeks or months between real rain events, which means a weakness that developed gradually often goes completely unnoticed until the pump is actually called on to work at full capacity. A worn float switch, a partially clogged intake screen, or a motor nearing the end of its service life all tend to reveal themselves precisely when demand peaks, not during a routine check.

The Power Outage Problem

The single most damaging failure scenario isn't the pump itself failing. It's a power outage during the exact storm that's raising groundwater, since a standard sump pump runs on household electricity and simply stops the moment power goes out. Delaware County storms severe enough to genuinely threaten a basement are also frequently the storms most likely to knock out power, which means these two risks are correlated rather than independent.

A battery backup system addresses this specific scenario directly, keeping the pump running through an outage using a charged battery reserve. For any homeowner who's experienced flooding tied to an outage before, this is worth genuine consideration rather than an optional upsell.

Signs Your Pump Is Already Struggling

A pump that runs constantly, rather than cycling on and off as water rises and falls, is either undersized for the actual water volume entering your basement or has a stuck float switch keeping it running unnecessarily. A pump making a new grinding, rattling, or straining sound is showing real mechanical wear worth investigating before it fails completely.

A pump that hasn't been tested in longer than you can remember is a genuine unknown, and an unknown is exactly what you don't want discovered during an actual storm.

Testing Your Pump the Right Way

Pour a full bucket of water directly into the sump pit and watch what happens. The float should rise, the pump should activate within a few seconds, and it should fully drain the pit before shutting off again. Hesitation, a delayed start, or water remaining in the pit after the pump stops are all signs worth addressing now rather than during the next real storm.

Why Delaware County's Geography Makes This a Real, Recurring Risk

Homes near the Olentangy River corridor, running through Powell, Lewis Center, and parts of Delaware itself, see genuine groundwater pressure during spring thaw and heavy rain. Galena, sitting at the literal headwaters of Hoover Reservoir where the Walnut Creeks converge, faces a similar dynamic from a different body of water. This isn't a hypothetical risk in this part of Ohio. It's a predictable, recurring seasonal pattern.

What to Do If Your Pump Fails Mid-Storm

Call immediately rather than waiting to see if the water recedes on its own. A basement actively flooding during a storm needs both the pump addressed and, depending on how much water has already entered, a water damage assessment before mold becomes a second problem layered on top of the first. Keep electronics and anything on the basement floor elevated on shelving year-round, so a sudden failure doesn't cause additional loss beyond the water itself, and never step into standing water near any electrical outlet or appliance.

Sizing Matters as Much as the Pump Itself

A pump that's technically working but undersized for your basement's actual water volume will run constantly during a real storm and may still fail to keep pace, giving a false sense of security right up until it doesn't. We size a replacement pump based on your basement's real square footage and how quickly water has historically entered it during past storms, not a single default size fitted to every home in the county.

Insurance Considerations Worth Understanding Now

Most standard homeowner policies do not automatically cover basement flooding from sump pump failure or groundwater, which surprises many homeowners after the fact. A specific water backup or sump failure endorsement, available as an add-on from many insurers, is worth asking about before you need it rather than discovering the gap during a claim.

Why Regular Maintenance Beats Reactive Repair

A pump inspected and cleaned annually, with the float switch, discharge line, and check valve all verified working, rarely fails without warning, since a technician catches developing wear before it becomes an actual failure during the storm that matters most. Treating sump pump maintenance the same way you'd treat furnace or water heater maintenance is genuinely worthwhile. It's a real annual task rather than something to think about only when it breaks, and it's the single most effective thing a Delaware County homeowner can do to avoid this exact scenario entirely.

What We Look at During a Sump Pump Inspection

Beyond a basic function test, a real inspection checks the pump's age against its expected service life, the condition of the discharge line both inside and outside the home, and whether the pit itself has accumulated debris that could interfere with the float switch's movement. Each of these is a real, specific thing that can quietly undermine an otherwise functional pump, and catching them early is exactly what separates routine maintenance from an emergency call during the next storm.

The Check Valve Nobody Thinks About

A missing or failed check valve on the discharge line lets water flow back down into the sump pit after each pump cycle, forcing the pump to immediately restart and do the same work twice. This hidden inefficiency can make a technically functional pump seem like it's struggling when the actual root cause is a simple, inexpensive valve replacement rather than the pump itself.

Common Questions About Sump Pump Failure

How often should I actually test my sump pump? Every few months at minimum, and always before the season when heavy rain or spring thaw is expected in Delaware County specifically.

Is a battery backup worth it if I've never had a power outage during a storm? It's genuinely worth considering anyway, since the correlation between severe storms and outages in this area is real, and the cost of a backup system is modest compared to a flooded, unpumped basement.

Can I install a backup pump myself? Some homeowners do attempt this, but proper sizing, discharge line integration, and battery capacity calculation are easy to get wrong, and a poorly installed backup provides false confidence rather than real protection.
