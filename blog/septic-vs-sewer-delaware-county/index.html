Septic vs. City Sewer in Delaware County

Written by the Delaware 24/7 Plumbing team · Updated October 12, 2026

Call(513) 796-9381

A homeowner moving to Delaware County from a fully sewered city often assumes every home connects to a municipal sewer system. That assumption causes real problems here, since a genuinely large share of the county, especially in rural townships like Scioto, Troy, and Orange outside their more developed pockets, still relies on private septic systems rather than a city connection.

How to Tell Which One You Actually Have

If you're unsure, check your property's title paperwork or ask your real estate agent directly before closing, since this affects your ongoing maintenance responsibility significantly. A visible septic tank lid or riser in the yard, or a drain field area that looks slightly different from the surrounding lawn, are both physical signs of a septic system.

A home inside Delaware's city limits or one of the more developed unincorporated areas near Lewis Center or Powell is more likely, though not guaranteed, to be on city sewer.

The Real Maintenance Difference

City sewer requires essentially no maintenance from the homeowner beyond keeping your own household drain lines clear. The city or water authority is responsible for the sewer main itself.

Septic is a different commitment entirely. A septic tank needs pumping on a real schedule, typically every 3 to 5 years depending on household size and tank capacity, and skipping that maintenance lets solids flow into the drain field, where they can cause expensive, sometimes irreversible damage.

A septic system also needs a working drain field with enough soil absorption capacity, which isn't something you can add more of later if the system was undersized for the property to begin with.

What New Owners Commonly Get Wrong

Assuming a septic system was properly maintained by the previous owner, without asking for pumping records, is a common and expensive mistake. A septic inspection before closing is genuinely worth requesting on any rural Delaware County property, the same way a home inspection covers the roof or foundation.

Flushing or draining things a septic system can't handle is another frequent issue for someone new to septic living. Flushable wipes, despite the label, don't break down the way toilet paper does. Cooking grease poured down a kitchen drain solidifies inside the tank and lines. Harsh chemical drain cleaners disrupt the bacterial balance the tank relies on to break down waste naturally.

None of these cause problems on city sewer the same way, which is exactly why someone switching from sewer to septic often doesn't think twice about habits that are genuinely risky for a septic system specifically.

What Happens If a Septic System Fails

A failing drain field is a significantly more expensive repair than routine pumping, sometimes requiring a full drain field replacement if the soil has become too saturated or compacted to properly absorb effluent. Staying current on pumping is what prevents that failure in the first place, since it's the accumulation of solids reaching the drain field that typically causes this kind of damage over time.

What a Real Septic Inspection Actually Checks

Beyond confirming the tank isn't overdue for pumping, a real inspection checks the condition of the inlet and outlet baffles, which direct flow correctly through the tank and are a common failure point on older systems. It also assesses the drain field's general condition, including any visible signs of saturation like unusually lush or soggy grass over the field even in dry weather.

For a property that's changed hands multiple times without documented septic service, a full inspection before you rely on the system daily is worth the modest cost. It either confirms everything is in good working order or surfaces a real issue while it's still manageable.

Living With Septic Long-Term

Once a homeowner adjusts habits around what goes down the drain and stays on a real pumping schedule, a well-maintained septic system is genuinely low-maintenance day to day. The upfront learning curve, mostly about what not to flush and staying ahead of the pumping schedule, is the real adjustment for someone coming from a lifetime on city sewer.

What Selling a Septic Property Involves

If you're the one selling a septic property in Delaware County, having a recent pumping and inspection on record helps considerably. Documentation you can hand to a buyer tends to smooth over a question that otherwise makes buyers nervous about a system they can't easily evaluate themselves. A buyer's agent or inspector will often ask specifically about septic maintenance history. Having a real answer ready avoids delays during a sale.

Township-by-Township Differences Worth Knowing

Rural townships like Scioto, Troy, and Orange each have their own mix of septic and any limited municipal service, and even within one township, one road might have city sewer access while a neighboring road relies entirely on septic. Never assume based on a neighboring property. Confirm your specific parcel's setup directly rather than guessing from what a nearby house appears to have.

What a Real Estate Agent May Not Tell You

Not every real estate agent has deep familiarity with septic systems, especially one who typically works more sewered, suburban listings. Asking directly, in writing, whether a specific property is on septic or sewer, and requesting any available service records, puts the responsibility on the seller's side to provide real documentation rather than leaving it to assumption during a fast-moving purchase.

A Final Word on Reassurance Versus Real Risk

A properly maintained septic system genuinely presents little day-to-day risk, and the real danger comes almost entirely from deferred maintenance rather than the technology itself being unreliable. Understanding this upfront, rather than treating septic as inherently more failure-prone than city sewer, sets realistic expectations for anyone new to it and for anyone who's lived with it for years already. Any septic or sewer work we perform follows the same state registration standard set by Ohio House Bill 487.

Delaware, Ohio Local Note: What the Health District Oversees

Delaware Public Health District reviews designs, inspects installations, and issues operation permits for household sewage treatment systems serving one, two, or three-family homes. Those systems are used where connection to a sanitary sewer is not feasible.

City sewer customers are served by the City of Delaware's wastewater division, which collects and treats wastewater and returns it to the Olentangy River. Call the district at (740) 368-1700 with permit and inspection questions before you buy or alter a septic system.

Common Questions About Septic and Sewer in Delaware County

Can a septic system be converted to city sewer?

In some areas, yes, if a sewer main is accessible nearby, though this is a significant project involving both the connection itself and properly decommissioning the old septic tank. It's worth discussing with the local utility authority for a specific property.

How do I know if my septic tank is due for pumping?

Slow drains throughout the house, a soggy patch over the drain field, or simply not knowing when it was last serviced are all reasons to schedule an inspection rather than guessing.

Is septic water safe for a garden or lawn?

A properly functioning drain field treats wastewater safely within the soil, but a failing or overflowing system is a genuine health concern, not just an inconvenience, and should be addressed immediately rather than left alone.

Who permits and inspects septic systems in Delaware County?

Delaware Public Health District reviews new system designs, inspects parcels and installations, and issues operation permits. Its environmental health staff schedule inspections at (740) 368-1700.
