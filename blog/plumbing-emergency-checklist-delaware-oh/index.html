A Delaware Homeowner's Plumbing Emergency Checklist

Written by the Delaware 24/7 Plumbing team · Emergency Guide

A plumbing emergency is stressful precisely because it demands quick decisions with no advance warning. Knowing exactly what to do, in what order, before we arrive genuinely limits damage and often reduces the eventual repair cost. This is the real checklist we'd want every Delaware County homeowner to have ready before they need it.

Step One: Find and Shut Off Your Main Water Valve

Every home has a main shutoff valve, and knowing its exact location before an emergency happens saves genuinely critical minutes during one. In most Delaware area homes, it's located where the water service line enters the house, often in a basement near the front foundation wall, in a utility closet, or in a crawlspace access point.

Turn the valve clockwise to shut off water flow to the entire house. If it's a lever-style ball valve, a quarter turn perpendicular to the pipe fully closes it. Test that you know how to operate your specific valve now, before an emergency, since a stuck or unfamiliar valve costs you real time during an actual crisis.

Step Two: Cut Power to Any Affected Electrical Area

If water is anywhere near an outlet, appliance, or electrical panel, shut off power to that specific area at the breaker before doing anything else. Never step into standing water that might be in contact with an energized outlet or appliance, even briefly, since this is a genuine electrocution risk that outweighs almost any other consideration in the moment.

Step Three: Contain What You Can Safely Contain

Move furniture, electronics, and anything easily damaged out of the affected area if you can do it safely without wading through standing water. Towels, buckets, and a wet-dry vacuum can slow the spread of a manageable leak while you wait, though a genuinely major leak or burst pipe is beyond what household containment can meaningfully address.

Step Four: Document Before You Clean Up

Photograph the damage, the water line on walls or flooring, and the source of the leak if it's identifiable, before extraction or cleanup begins. This documentation matters significantly if you file a homeowner's insurance claim, and it's much harder to reconstruct convincingly after the fact.

Step Five: Call and Describe What You're Seeing Clearly

When you call, describe the situation as specifically as you can. Whether water is actively flowing or has stopped, which room and roughly where in that room, whether you've already shut off the main valve, and whether the water appears clean or contains sewage all help us dispatch appropriately. That level of detail lets us arrive prepared with the right equipment for your specific situation.

Situations That Need a Different First Step

A gas smell changes this entire checklist. Leave the house immediately without touching any switches, then call your gas utility's emergency line from outside before calling us. A sewage backup means avoiding all contact with the water, since it carries real bacterial contamination risk, and keeping children and pets away from the affected area entirely.

Building Your Own Household Emergency Card

Write down your main shutoff valve's exact location, our phone number, and your water heater's shutoff location on a card kept somewhere accessible, like inside a kitchen cabinet or taped near your electrical panel. During an actual emergency, stress makes it harder to think clearly, and having this information already written down removes one variable from an already difficult moment.

Know Your Individual Fixture Shutoffs Too

Beyond the main valve, most toilets, sinks, and washing machines have their own dedicated shutoff valve, usually a small oval or round handle on the supply line beneath or behind the fixture. Knowing these locations lets you isolate a single problem fixture without shutting off water to the entire house, which matters when the emergency is contained to one bathroom or the kitchen rather than affecting your whole home's plumbing.

Test each fixture shutoff periodically, since a valve that's never been turned can seize from mineral deposits over years, especially given Delaware's real water hardness. Discovering a frozen shutoff valve during an actual emergency, when you need it to work immediately, is a genuinely bad time to learn it doesn't turn.

Why We Take Every Call Seriously, Even Uncertain Ones

If you're genuinely unsure whether a situation qualifies as an emergency, call anyway rather than waiting to see if it gets worse. A dispatcher can quickly help you assess the situation over the phone, and it's always better to have that conversation than to wait through the night on something that turns out to have needed immediate attention.

What to Keep Near Your Main Shutoff Valve

A basic wrench sized for your specific valve, if it's not a lever-style ball valve, saves time if the handle is stiff or hard to grip by hand alone. A flashlight matters too, since plumbing emergencies don't wait for daylight and many shutoff valves are located in basements or crawlspaces with poor lighting even under normal conditions.

After the Immediate Emergency Is Handled

Once water is shut off and the immediate danger has passed, resist the urge to immediately start cleanup before we've had a chance to assess the full situation. Some water damage isn't visible right away, and a technician can often identify secondary risks, like moisture that's reached a wall cavity, that aren't obvious from a quick visual check alone.

Preventing the Next Emergency

Many plumbing emergencies trace back to a warning sign that went unnoticed or ignored for weeks or months beforehand, whether that's a slow leak, an aging water heater, or a pipe that's frozen once before. A periodic walkthrough of your home's plumbing, checking visible pipes, testing shutoff valves, and noting anything unusual, catches real problems while they're still manageable rather than waiting for them to become the next emergency call. Any repair work we perform follows the state registration standard set by Ohio House Bill 487.

Common Questions About Plumbing Emergencies

How do I know if something is a real emergency versus something that can wait? Active flooding, a burst pipe, sewage backup, or a gas smell are genuine emergencies needing immediate attention. A slow drip, a running toilet, or a minor clog can typically wait for a scheduled appointment.

What if I can't find my main shutoff valve? Call us and describe your basement or utility area, and we can often help you locate it over the phone while you wait, or we'll shut it off ourselves upon arrival if the situation is urgent enough.

Should I try to fix a burst pipe myself before help arrives? No. Focus entirely on the shutoff steps above rather than attempting a repair, since an improper temporary fix can sometimes make the eventual professional repair more complicated.
