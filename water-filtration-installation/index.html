Filtration and Softening Solve Different Problems

A water softener addresses hardness, the calcium and magnesium common in Delaware's water supply. A filtration system addresses taste, odor, sediment, and chlorine, which are different issues entirely. Many homes genuinely benefit from both installed together, but they are not interchangeable, and installing one when you need the other leaves the actual complaint unresolved.

"Water filtration installation" covers whole-house sediment and carbon filtration, point-of-use systems like an under-sink drinking water filter, and in some cases reverse osmosis for a specific tap. We test what's actually in your water before recommending which type solves your specific concern.

Common Reasons Delaware Homeowners Call About Filtration

A chlorine taste or smell from municipal water is one of the most common complaints, and a whole-house carbon filter addresses it at the point where water enters the home rather than only at one faucet. Visible sediment or cloudiness, more common on well water in rural parts of the county, points toward a sediment pre-filter rather than a carbon or reverse osmosis system.

Some homeowners want filtered water specifically for drinking and cooking without filtering the entire house, which is where a dedicated under-sink or reverse osmosis system at the kitchen tap makes more sense than a whole-house install.

What We Test Before Recommending a System

We check for chlorine, sediment, and, on well water specifically, iron and sulfur content, since each of those has a different correct filtration approach. A system sized for chlorine taste alone won't meaningfully help with sediment, and the reverse is also true. This installation work follows the same state registration standard set by Ohio House Bill 487 as any other plumbing work we perform.

Filter Maintenance Nobody Mentions Until It's a Problem

A carbon or sediment filter needs its cartridge replaced on a real schedule, typically every 3 to 6 months depending on your household's water use and the specific contaminant load, not left in place indefinitely. A cartridge left too long doesn't just stop filtering effectively, it can become a place where bacteria accumulate, which defeats the purpose of having filtered water in the first place.

Reverse osmosis systems need periodic membrane replacement on a longer cycle, usually every 2 to 3 years, along with more frequent pre-filter changes. We walk through the specific maintenance schedule for whatever system we install so you know what to expect rather than being surprised later.
